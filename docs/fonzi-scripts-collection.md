### Script 33

**Title:** Why Your Perfect Resume Is Costing You Interviews

**Script:**

Your perfectly written resume might actually be making it harder for you to get hired. And the reason has nothing to do with your experience... It has everything to do with AI. To understand why, we need to rewind to the old job market. Back then, an effective job search was pretty simple. You found five to fifteen roles, carefully tailored your resume to each job description, and tried to look like the perfect candidate. Recruiters would review the applications, surface the strongest people, and invite them to interview. Networking helped, of course... But it wasn't always necessary. If your resume matched the role and your experience looked strong, you had a real shot.

Then AI entered the chat, and the entire system broke. Suddenly, anyone could copy a job description, paste it into AI, and generate a perfectly tailored resume in seconds. Then they could repeat that process for fifty or even one hundred jobs. But here's where it gets interesting. When hundreds of people use the exact same job description to generate their resumes, they start using suspiciously similar language. The same polished sentences. The same keywords. The same generic claims about being a "results-driven leader" with a "proven track record." Meanwhile, recruiters are buried under more applications than ever, trying to figure out who's genuinely qualified and who's simply good at prompting AI.

So how do you stand out when everyone suddenly sounds perfect? Specificity. Be specific about the problems you solved, the decisions you made, the work you produced, and the results you created. Make your cover letter sound like it could only have been written by you. Show real examples. Build a personal brand around something clear and memorable. And make sure the people in your network can explain exactly what you're great at. Because in the age of AI, polished language is cheap. Proof is valuable. Your resume shouldn't read like the perfect answer to a job description... It should read like evidence that there's only one person who could have written it.

**Title:** The AI Model Is the Engine. The Harness Is the Moat.

**Script:**

Everyone keeps asking the same question. Which AI model is the smartest? But that is like asking which engine would win a race before putting it inside a car. An engine cannot steer, brake, read the road, or recover when something goes wrong. It needs an entire system built around it. In AI, that system is called the harness. The model provides intelligence. But the harness turns logic into actual work. It chooses the context, connects tools like GitHub and the terminal, controls what the model can touch, runs its commands, checks the result, and sends it back to try again when it fails. Codex, Claude Code, and Cursor are the harnesses that make Sol, Fable, and other models useful. Each wraps a model in different tools, permissions, memory, prompts, and feedback loops to drive vastly different results.

Back in April, Anthropic showed why the rest of the car matters. It previewed Mythos, a new model that could find and exploit software vulnerabilities. Anthropic considered it too dangerous for a public release and gave selected organizations early access for defensive work. Visa was one of them. But Visa did not simply point Mythos at its code and tell it to start hunting. It built an eleven-stage harness around the model. One stage maps the attack surface. Another models possible threats. Specialist agents search for vulnerabilities, while separate agents try to prove every finding is real. Then the system removes duplicates, scores the results, proposes fixes, and sends those fixes through an adversarial validation panel. The model provides the reasoning and finds the bugs. The harness runs the security system.

And by July, the same pattern was appearing across the industry. Shopify built Dispatch to divide massive codebases, send security agents out in parallel, and prove every finding with real tests. Coinbase turned internal agents into monitored software graphs with evaluations, audit trails, fixed steps, and human approval gates. Ramp built infrastructure that could route work between models based on cost, quality, and difficulty. Then, on August 27, Visa updated its harness so different stages could use Anthropic, OpenAI, or compatible open-weight models. Meaning the engine could be replaced without rebuilding the entire car. And that may be the real durable advantage. Models will keep getting cheaper and smarter. One will take the lead, and another will eventually leapfrog it. But the harness is where a company stores its process, permissions, context, verification, and judgment. The model is the engine. The harness is everything the company learns about how to drive.
